# QO-100, and satellites without a licence

Two ways an XPRS station can reach a satellite. One is free of money and needs a
licence; the other needs neither, and most people do not know it exists.

Status: RESEARCH. Nothing here is implemented. See [spectrum.md](spectrum.md)
for the bands XPRS actually proposes to use.

---

## 1. What QO-100 is

Es'hail-2 is a Qatari communications satellite, operated by Es'hailSat, sitting
in geostationary orbit at 25.9 degrees east. It carries the world's first
geostationary amateur radio transponders, known as QO-100 (Qatar-OSCAR 100) and
coordinated by AMSAT-DL.

It does not move. Point an antenna once and it stays pointed, which is the whole
difference between this and every other amateur satellite.

| Transponder | Uplink | Downlink | Width |
|---|---|---|---|
| Narrowband | 2400.050 - 2400.300 MHz | 10489.550 - 10489.800 MHz | 250 kHz |
| Wideband | 2401.500 - 2409.500 MHz | 10491.000 - 10499.000 MHz | 8 MHz |

Uplink is right-hand circular, downlink vertical linear. Recommended uplink is
**25 W EIRP** -- 5 W into a 7 dBi antenna. On the narrowband transponder each
signal is limited to **2.7 kHz**.

Coverage is about a third of the planet: Brazil eastward through Europe, Africa,
the Middle East and most of Asia to Thailand. Not the Pacific, not the western
Americas, not Australia or New Zealand, nothing polar. One antenna, one fixed
aim, a third of the world, and a hard edge.

## 2. Free of money, not free of paperwork

There is no subscription, no SIM, no operator and no company that can switch you
off. There is an amateur licence requirement, because this is the amateur
satellite service.

Everything in section 9.4 of [XPRS.md](XPRS.md) applies unchanged: the
operator's own callsign, never a self-generated `X1` or `X3` one, and **no
encryption**.

That last point is regulatory rather than technical. The transponder is a linear
bent pipe and would relay ciphertext perfectly well; ITU Radio Regulations
article 25.2A prohibits codes intended to obscure meaning, and the amateur
satellite service is covered by it.

| | On QO-100 |
|---|---|
| `g:` signature | permitted, and recommended |
| `x:` sealed body | never |

A signature is not encryption. It leaves the text in clear and establishes only
authorship, which is exactly what should be used here.

**A gateway between the licence-free mesh and QO-100 must refuse to forward
sealed packets.** XPRS section 9.4 already requires this; it was written for APRS
bridging and covers this case unchanged.

The consequence is worth stating plainly: QO-100 is a **public** bearer. It suits
`t:warning`, `t:info`, `t:blog` and open messages across a continent, and it is
structurally unsuitable as a backhaul for private mail.

## 3. Hardware

### 3.1 What does not work

**An ESP32 cannot be used**, and the reason is not power. Power is the one part
that is fine: 20 dBm into a 24 dBi dish is 25 W EIRP, exactly what is asked for.

| Objection | Detail |
|---|---|
| Cannot tune there | The lowest frequency an ESP32 emits on is BLE channel 37 at 2402.0 MHz, which is 1.7 MHz above the top of the passband |
| Wrong shape by orders of magnitude | BLE is 1 MHz wide, 370x the permitted 2.7 kHz; WiFi is 20 MHz, 7400x. One BLE packet is four times wider than the entire transponder |
| Not stable enough | A good board is +/-10 ppm, which at 2400 MHz is 24 kHz of drift against a 2.7 kHz channel, nine times too much |

The second objection is the fatal one. It is not a tuning problem that better
firmware could solve: a single transmission would wipe out every contact across a
third of the planet at once.

### 3.2 What works

An SDR. **HackRF can be made to work but is the wrong choice**, and an
**ADALM-Pluto is better** for three concrete reasons: it is full duplex, so it
solves in one box the requirement to hear your own downlink while transmitting;
it has a 12-bit converter against HackRF's 8, which matters when picking a weak
signal out of a shared 250 kHz transponder; and it has a TCXO already.

HackRF specifically needs an external 10 MHz reference. Its stock clock is
+/-20 ppm, which is 48 kHz of drift against a 2.7 kHz channel -- the same trap as
the ESP32, except that HackRF has a CLKIN port and a GPSDO fixes it completely.

A station either way:

| Part | Why |
|---|---|
| ADALM-Pluto, or HackRF plus a GPSDO | the radio and its reference |
| 2.4 GHz PA, 2-5 W | HackRF gives about 10 dBm; a 20 dBi dish still needs 14 dB of gain to reach 25 W EIRP |
| Modified LNB, 25 MHz reference | 10489 MHz is above any of these SDRs, so the downlink is converted to about 740 MHz |
| 60-90 cm offset dish, dual feed | both bands, one aim point |
| Second receiver, if using HackRF | half duplex cannot hear itself |

### 3.3 On a boat

The blocker is pointing. Geostationary means aiming at one fixed spot in the sky
and staying there, and a 60 cm dish at 10.5 GHz has roughly 3 degrees of
beamwidth. Any roll loses the downlink. A stabilised marine mount costs more than
the entire radio.

At anchor it is straightforward. Under way it is not.

## 4. Digital modes and file transfer

The narrowband transponder carries CW, PSK31, RTTY, FT8, SSTV, narrowband
digital voice and packet-style data. A 250-byte XPRS packet fits comfortably.

**AX.25 is not the answer here.** Section 8.1 of [spectrum.md](spectrum.md)
proposes AX.25 for VHF, and its entire justification is that thirty years of
existing TNCs and digipeaters work unmodified. That argument does not reach a
satellite, and AX.25 is poor on its own merits: 1200 baud, no forward error
correction, and stop-and-wait ARQ that collapses on a marginal link.

For file transfer, use the other transponder. The wideband transponder carries
DVB-S2 -- modern, LDPC-coded, adaptive, and with cheap silicon because it is the
satellite television standard.

| Symbol rate | QPSK 1/2 | QPSK 3/4 | 8PSK 2/3 | |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 125 kS/s | 124 kbps | 186 kbps | 248 kbps | modest dish |
| 333 kS/s | 329 kbps | 495 kbps | 659 kbps | |
| 500 kS/s | 494 kbps | 744 kbps | 990 kbps | most stations stop here |
| 1000 kS/s | 989 kbps | 1487 kbps | 1980 kbps | |
| 1500 kS/s | 1484 kbps | 2230 kbps | 2970 kbps | maximum routine, big dish |

What that does to a transfer:

| | 250 B XPRS packet | 10 MB file |
|---|---|---|
| 125 kS/s QPSK 1/2 | 16.13 ms | 645 s |
| 500 kS/s QPSK 1/2 | 4.05 ms | 162 s |
| 1.5 MS/s 8PSK 2/3 | 0.67 ms | 27 s |
| narrowband, VARA-class 2.4 kbps | 833 ms | 9.3 hours |
| narrowband, 1200 baud AFSK | 1.67 s | 18.5 hours |

Ten megabytes in under three minutes rather than nine hours.

**XPRS needs no change to use this.** Section 6.7 of [XPRS.md](XPRS.md) defines
`file:` as a content hash and says outright that how the bytes travel is outside
the specification. So the packet naming the file goes over LoRa or the narrowband
transponder, and the bytes come over DVB-S2 on the wideband one. The reference
stays valid either way.

Two caveats. The wideband transponder needs considerably more EIRP, which is why
most stations stop at 500 kS/s. And DVB-S2 is a broadcast standard with no return
path, so file transfer over it needs fountain coding or an out-of-band
acknowledgement; TCP will not work.

One detail worth noting: LoRa chirp spread spectrum experiments already run
through QO-100 at 125 kHz. The same modulation family as the terrestrial mesh,
through a geostationary satellite.

## 5. Jamming and congestion

Badly, and by construction. A linear transponder has no way to refuse anyone.

Everything that exists is cooperative:

- **Beacons** at both edges of the passband, from AMSAT-DL at Bochum. They set
  the reference: keep your downlink no stronger than the beacon.
- **LEILA**, automated monitoring that detects excessive signals and transmits a
  warning back at the offender.
- **Full duplex is mandatory**, so that you can see your own signal.
- Community pressure, which on QO-100 is real.

All of it assumes an operator who wants to comply.

The transponder shares one fixed output power among everything present, which is
why power discipline matters so much:

| Signals present | Share each |
|---|---|
| 1 | 0 dB |
| 2 | -3 dB |
| 4 | -6 dB |
| 10 | -10 dB |
| 25 | -14 dB |
| 50 | -17 dB |

**One station running 10 dB too hot consumes the power of ten polite ones.** That
is the arithmetic behind the beacons and LEILA, not etiquette.

Capacity is 250 kHz over 2.7 kHz per signal: about **93 slots in theory**, fewer
after beacon guard bands. That is the entire allowance for a third of the planet.

Against deliberate jamming there is no defence. No authentication, no access
control, and geostationary geometry makes direction finding very hard because
every uplink arrives at the same antenna from nearly the same direction. The
operator's only lever is switching the transponder off, which punishes everyone.

**What that means for XPRS.** A signature proves who wrote a packet and does
nothing to keep a channel open. No format fixes a shared unarbitrated medium.

The answer is not per-bearer robustness but bearer diversity and carrying. A
jammer can deny QO-100; it cannot simultaneously deny LoRa, BLE, WiFi, the
internet and a boat sailing to Athens with the packet in its store. Section 13.4
of [XPRS.md](XPRS.md) exists for the case where no channel is available at all,
and jamming is one more instance of that.

So: **treat QO-100 as an opportunistic broadcast bearer, never a dependency.**

## 6. LoRa to a satellite, with no licence at all

This is the part most people do not know, and for XPRS it matters more than
everything above.

**A licence-exempt SRD transmitting within ISM limits does not stop being legal
because a satellite hears it.** Transmission is regulated by where you transmit
from, on what frequency, at what power and duty cycle. Nothing in the short-range
device rules says the receiver must be on the ground.

This is not a loophole being argued theoretically. Lacuna Space runs a commercial
direct-to-satellite LoRaWAN constellation on 868 and 915 MHz using ordinary
LoRaWAN end devices, and **ECC Report 357**, approved in June 2024, specifically
addresses devices communicating direct to satellites in 862-870 MHz. Germany
authorised Lacuna in 2020.

The licence that is required belongs to the **satellite operator** -- ITU
coordination and a space station authorisation. Not to the person on the ground.

| | Ground station licence | Reaches |
|---|---|---|
| Receiving any satellite | none, anywhere | everything overhead |
| LoRa uplink to a LEO satellite, within ISM limits | **none** | a passing satellite |
| QO-100 | amateur licence | a third of the planet, continuously |

And it closes physically. Taking SF12 at 125 kHz, a satellite antenna of about
6 dBi and a receiver at -137 dBm:

| Ground power | Budget | 550 km overhead | 1000 km mid-pass | 2000 km low elevation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 25 mW, EU general SRD | 159 dB | +13.0 dB | +7.8 dB | +1.8 dB |
| 500 mW, EU 869.4-869.65 | 172 dB | +26.0 dB | +20.8 dB | +14.8 dB |

Twenty-five milliwatts reaches low Earth orbit with margin. That is why Lacuna
and Fossa exist.

For comparison, QO-100 in geostationary orbit is 35786 km away and the path loss
at 2400 MHz is **191 dB** -- which is the entire reason it needs a dish and a
power amplifier, and a LEO cubesat does not.

### 6.1 What this costs

LEO satellites pass. There is no continuous link, only windows of a few minutes
several times a day, and the data rate at SF12 is a few tens of bits per second
-- tens of bytes per pass, not kilobytes.

Which suits XPRS unusually well. A packet is 250 bytes, expiry is already a field
(`until:`), and the format is built around delivery that may take a while
(section 13.4 of [XPRS.md](XPRS.md)). A satellite that collects packets on one
pass and drops them on another is a **carrier** in exactly the sense the format
already defines -- one that happens to move at 7 km/s.

### 6.2 What is actually flying today

The legal path and the physics both work. What is missing is a satellite willing
to listen, and that is a cubesat rather than a treaty.

**Receiving is free, unlicensed and already large.** TinyGS is an open ground
station network, started in 2019 to catch FossaSat-1, now more than 8000 stations
that have logged over 65 million packets. The hardware is an ESP32 and a LoRa
module, about 20 euros. It decodes LoRa, FSK, GFSK, MSK, GMSK and OOK, and during
2026 has been receiving FossaSat-2E26 and PROVES-Electra.

No licence is required to receive anything, anywhere. If satellites are wanted in
this project quickly, this is the door that is already open.

**Transmitting without a licence has no free satellite.** ECC Report 357 makes
the uplink lawful and 25 mW closes the link, but the constellations built on that
basis are commercial. Nobody is flying an open satellite that accepts arbitrary
licence-exempt uplinks. That is a business gap, not a legal or physical one.

**Transmitting with an amateur licence has roughly 80 to 120 active satellites**,
and one of them matters unusually much here.

### 6.3 The ISS digipeater, which needs no new hardware

The International Space Station carries an APRS digipeater on **145.825 MHz,
1200 baud AFSK, AX.25**.

That is exactly the link layer section 8.1 of [spectrum.md](spectrum.md) already
proposes for VHF. **An XPRS station built for terrestrial APRS works the ISS with
no hardware change at all** -- same radio, same TNC, same modem, pointed at the
sky instead of a hilltop.

| | |
|---|---|
| Footprint radius | 2252 km, about 40 degrees of the globe at once |
| Pass length | roughly 10 minutes, 4 to 6 usable passes a day |
| Capacity | around 120 XPRS packets per pass at one third channel use |

Link margin from a 5 W handheld with a 2 dBi whip:

| Elevation | Slant range | Margin |
|---|---|---|
| overhead | 420 km | 30.8 dB |
| 30 degrees | 774 km | 25.5 dB |
| 10 degrees | 1492 km | 19.8 dB |
| 5 degrees | 1861 km | 17.9 dB |

Eighteen decibels in hand at the horizon, from a handheld and a piece of wire. It
is the most accessible satellite in existence, and for a format whose packets are
250 bytes it is a genuine carrier: a station under the footprint hands a packet
up, another station 4000 km away on the same pass takes it down.

The amateur rules still bind. Own callsign, plaintext only, no `x:`.

**IO-117 (GreenCube) is dead**, from radiation damage in medium Earth orbit. It
was the best digital satellite for traffic of this kind and it is a real loss.
SO-50, FO-29 and the TEVEL FM constellation remain.

### 6.4 The honest comparison

| | Licence | Direction | Cost | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| TinyGS and LoRa satellites | none | receive only | about 20 euros | working now |
| ISS APRS digipeater | amateur | both | nothing, if you have VHF | working now |
| QO-100 | amateur | both | dish, PA, SDR | working now |
| LoRa uplink to LEO | none | both | about 20 euros | **no open satellite exists** |
| Lacuna and other commercial | none | both | subscription | working now |
| Carry by hand or boat | none | both | nothing | working now |

QO-100 is the better radio and excludes anyone without a licence. Direct-to-LEO
is slow, intermittent and open to everybody, which is the constraint this project
actually optimises for.

Neither is implemented. If one were built first, it should be the second.
